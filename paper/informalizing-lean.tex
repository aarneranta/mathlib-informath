\begin{abstract}
Lean and its library Mathlib contain a large and growing part of mathematics in machine-checked
form, but the formal code is hard to read for anyone who is not a Lean expert, and the code
produced by autoformalization is read by even fewer. We describe a system that translates Lean
declarations, with their statements, definitions and proofs, into ordinary mathematical
language in several natural languages. The translation goes through the logical framework
Dedukti and the Informath grammar, written in Grammatical Framework (GF), and is symbolic: it is
compositional and does not invent content. The main bottleneck is the symbol table, which tells
how each Lean constant is to be worded. We infer it for all of Mathlib's definitions from
their docstrings and names, checking each candidate with the grammar and replacing the words
that it lacks by words of its lexicon: 69\% of 38,667 definitions get a wording, and Mathlib's
own notation gives symbolic variants. Mathlib's classes, with their multiple inheritance, are
read as a textbook defines algebraic structures, by extension of one parent (``a group is a
monoid $G$ with an operation ${}^{-1}$ on $G$ such that \ldots''), and as predicates of their
carriers. All definitions are then read in English, as formulas and in French, with their
definientia: over 99\% get a reading in English and in French, with the same symbol table,
almost a third of them with no constant left symbolic. Theorems and proofs are read for
9,126 declarations of 82 modules, the undergraduate core of Mathlib from logic and orders to
algebra, number theory, linear algebra, topology and analysis, in English, French and Czech:
72\% are fully worded, and 7,594 of the 8,472 theorems get a proof text, by terms or by
tactics. We compare the translation with lean2dk, which translates Lean to Dedukti for checking
rather than for reading, and show how the comparison led us to keep implicit arguments and
coercions in Dedukti and hide them only in the readings.
The system was built in a collaboration between a human researcher and an AI coding agent, which
is also a coauthor of this paper; we report what came from where.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction: the documentation problem of formal code}

Formal mathematics is precise but not readable. A theorem in Lean's Mathlib
\cite{mathlib-2020} is stated in a language designed for an elaborator, which resolves implicit
arguments, type classes, coercions and notations; a proof is a term or a script of tactics whose
meaning lies in the goals that only Lean displays. Mathlib has a documentation tradition that
partly compensates for this: definitions must have docstrings, and doc-gen4 \cite{doc-gen4}
renders the declarations with their types. But in our checkout of Mathlib, 83\% of definitions,
structures and classes have a docstring, and only 15\% of theorems.

The problem becomes urgent with \emph{autoformalization}. Large language models now produce
formal mathematics at a scale that no human reviewer can follow \cite{urban-2026}. A proof
checker guarantees that the code is correct, but not that it formalizes what was meant: the
theorem proved may be a different theorem. To check this, someone has to read the formal
statements. An LLM can be asked to explain them, but then the explanation is no more reliable
than the LLM \cite{jiang-al-2024,gao-al-2025}.

\emph{Symbolic informalization} is the complement: a compiler-like translation from the formal
language to natural language, which follows the formal structure and adds nothing that is not
there \cite{ranta-2024}. Its traditional weaknesses are fluency, the effort of building it,
and the restriction to one formal language \cite{jiang-al-2024}. The Informath project
\cite{informath-homepage} addresses them with an interlingual architecture: Dedukti
\cite{assaf-al-2023} as the hub of formal languages and the Grammatical Framework (GF)
\cite{ranta-2004,ranta-2011} for natural languages.

This paper applies Informath to Lean and Mathlib. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item an exporter from Lean 4 declarations, statements, definitions and proofs to Informath's
  Dedukti, and a pipeline that reads them in English, French, Czech, German and Swedish, as
  well as in \LaTeX\ formulas, with term proofs and tactic proofs as texts
  (Section~\ref{sec:pipeline});
\item a method for inferring the symbol table, the mapping of Lean constants to words, for all
  of Mathlib's definitions from their docstrings and names, with each candidate checked by the
  grammar, and with Mathlib's notation as symbolic variants (Section~\ref{sec:tables});
\item a treatment of Mathlib's classes as algebraic structures defined by extension of a
  parent, and as defined predicates, with the GF judgements that read them
  (Section~\ref{sec:structures});
\item a comparison with lean2dk, a translation of Lean to Dedukti for checking, and the
  consequences we drew from it: implicit arguments and coercions kept in Dedukti and hidden
  by the symbol table (Section~\ref{sec:lean2dk});
\item an account of how much comes from the existing grammar, from Mathlib's own text, from
  general lexical resources, from the human author and from the AI agent (Section~\ref{sec:who});
\item results on all of Mathlib's definitions and on the theorems of a part of Mathlib, examples
  good and bad, and the open problems
  (Sections~\ref{sec:examples}--\ref{sec:problems}).
\end{itemize}
The system is open source \cite{mathlib-informath}, with web pages showing all 38,667
definitions with their readings in English, as formulas and in French, the 9,126 statements and
structures with their proofs in English, French and Czech, and a stand-alone program that
informalizes any Lean file.

\paragraph{Authorship.} The software described here and this paper were written by Claude, an
AI model by Anthropic, working as a coding agent (Claude Code \cite{claude-code}) in the first
author's checkout, in dialogue with him. The first author set the goals and directions,
designed Informath before this project, made the decisions between alternatives, diagnosed

\section{Lean and Mathlib: tool, resource, and test case}

Lean 4 \cite{demoura-al-2015,demoura-ullrich-2021} is a proof assistant and functional
programming language based on dependent type theory. Mathlib, its community library, covers
mathematics from algebra and number theory to analysis, topology and category theory; at the
commit we used (\ttt{6972d52c0d}, 26 October 2026) it has about 8,660 files and 2.3 million
lines, with about 194,000 theorems and 48,000 definitions, structures and classes.

Mathlib plays three roles for informalization. It is a \emph{tool}: Lean's elaborator resolves
what the source leaves implicit, and its environment gives each declaration its fully
elaborated type, which is what we translate. It is a \emph{resource}: its names follow strict
conventions (\ttt{Nat.Prime.two\_le}, \ttt{IsPrimePow}, \ttt{minFac}), and its docstrings are
written in mathematical English (``\ttt{Nat.Prime p} means that \ttt{p} is a prime number''),
so that both can be mined for wordings. And it is a \emph{test case}: it is large and varied
enough that no hand-written table can cover it, and its users are an audience who can judge
the results.

\section{GF, Dedukti, and Informath}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{simplified-informath.png}
\caption{The architecture of Informath: Dedukti as the formal interlingua, GF as the informal one.}
\label{fig:arch}
\end{figure}

Informath (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}) has two interlinguas. On the formal side, \textbf{Dedukti}
\cite{assaf-al-2023} is a minimal logical framework, the $\lambda\Pi$-calculus modulo rewriting,
designed as a hub between proof systems. Informath uses a fixed Dedukti theory of sets,
propositions and proofs:
\begin{lstlisting}
Set : Type.   Elem : Set -> Type.   Prop : Type.   Proof : Prop -> Type.
and : Prop -> Prop -> Prop.   forall : (A : Set) -> (Elem A -> Prop) -> Prop.
\end{lstlisting}
Constants beyond these base constants are user constants, such as the ones of Mathlib.

On the informal side, \textbf{GF} \cite{ranta-2004,ranta-2011} is a grammar formalism with an
\emph{abstract syntax}, a system of typed trees, and a \emph{concrete syntax} for each language,
which maps the trees to strings and back. Informath's abstract syntax has categories such as
\ttt{Prop}, \ttt{Kind}, \ttt{Exp}, \ttt{Proof}, and lexical categories such as \ttt{Noun},
\ttt{Adj}, \ttt{Fun} (``the X of Y''), \ttt{Verb2}; its concrete syntaxes are built with GF's
Resource Grammar Library (RGL) \cite{ranta-2009}, which implements inflection, agreement and
word order for some 40 languages, so that a rule such as ``$x$ is $A$'' is written once with
\ttt{mkCl} and gets the right forms in each.

The mapping between the two is defined by a \textbf{symbol table} (\ttt{.dkgf}), which assigns
to each Dedukti constant a wording, either a GF tree or an example string that the grammar
parses:
\begin{lstlisting}
Nat__minFac : "the smallest prime factor of #1" | $\operatorname{minFac}(#1)$
IsPrimePow  : "#1 is a prime power"
Eqt         : "#2 is equal to #3"     -- the type argument #1 is not shown
\end{lstlisting}
The string is parsed in a category \ttt{Example}, whose trees reveal the lexical category
(\ttt{Fun}, \ttt{Noun1}, \ttt{Adj2}, \ldots) and the order of the arguments. From the table,
a Dedukti statement is translated to a GF tree in a core language (MathCore), then rewritten by
natural language generation (NLG) rules into variants (in situ quantification, aggregation of
hypotheses, symbolic notation where it fits), which are ranked and linearized
\cite{ranta-2024}. A constant without an entry is shown symbolically, as
$\mathrm{IsUnit}(u)$.

\section{Prior work}

Readable formal mathematics has a long history. Mizar \cite{bancerek-al-2015} was designed to
look like mathematical text and produces the journal \emph{Formalized Mathematics}. Coscoy et
al.\ \cite{coscoy-al-1995} extracted texts from Coq (now Rocq) proof terms, which is close to
what we do for Lean's term proofs. Isabelle/Isar \cite{wenzel-1999} is a readable proof
language; Verbose Lean \cite{massot-2024} gives Lean a controlled natural language for
teaching, in which tactic proofs read like texts, and inspired our treatment of tactic proofs;
Section~\ref{sec:verbose} compares the two.
On the formal side, lean2dk \cite{lean2dk,vaishnav-2026} translates Lean to Dedukti for
checking; Section~\ref{sec:lean2dk} compares it with our translation.
In the other direction, controlled natural languages such as Naproche \cite{cramer-al-2009,
koepke-2025}, ForTheL \cite{paskevich-2007} and MathNat \cite{humayoun-raffalli-2010} are
parsed into formal languages. GF has been used for mathematical language from the start
\cite{hallgren-ranta-2000,ranta-2011c,kelber-al-2025}, and GFLean \cite{pathak-2024}
autoformalizes a fragment of English into Lean with GF.

Neural approaches have used LLMs to informalize Lean, notably the Herald dataset of Mathlib
statements annotated in natural language \cite{gao-al-2025}, and to autoformalize, with
symbolically generated data as one source \cite{jiang-al-2024,huang-al-2025}. Our work is
complementary: the translation itself is symbolic, but the lexicon that it needs is inferred
from Mathlib's own text, and the system was built with an AI agent.

\section{From Lean code to natural language}
\label{sec:pipeline}

The pipeline has four steps.

\paragraph{1. Export to Dedukti.} A Lean program, \ttt{Export.lean}, imports Mathlib modules and
translates each declaration's elaborated type, and the definiens of definitions and term proofs,
to Informath's Dedukti. A binder $(x : \alpha)$ with $\alpha : \ttt{Type}$ becomes
$(x : \ttt{Elem}\ \alpha)$, one with $\alpha : \ttt{Prop}$ a hypothesis, and
$(\alpha : \ttt{Type}\ u)$ becomes $(\alpha : \ttt{Set})$. Implicit arguments are kept,
and the symbol table hides them in the readings (see below). An instance
binder \ttt{[CommMonoid M]} becomes a hypothesis \ttt{Proof (CommMonoid M)}, a class is
declared as a predicate (and defined as one, with its structure, in
Section~\ref{sec:structures}), and a bundled function applied by coercion (\ttt{DFunLike.coe f x})
becomes a constant \ttt{f\_\_app} with its own type. A table of 39 mappings sends Lean's logic
and arithmetic (\ttt{Dvd.dvd}, \ttt{Nat.Prime}, \ttt{HAdd.hAdd}, \ttt{Iff}) to Informath's base
constants, whose wordings are part of Informath. The output is meant to be read, not checked:
it has no universe levels, computation rules or (but where a statement gives them, see below)
instance arguments, which a type checker would need (Section~\ref{sec:lean2dk}).
For example, the Lean theorem
\begin{lstlisting}
theorem Irreducible.not_dvd_isUnit {M : Type*} [CommMonoid M] {p u : M}
    (hp : Irreducible p) (hu : IsUnit u) : ¬p ∣ u
\end{lstlisting}
is exported with its implicit variables and its instance as hypotheses, as a theorem is stated
with all of them, and its proof term with them as $\lambda$s:
\begin{lstlisting}
def Irreducible__not_dvd_isUnit : (M : Set) -> (_ : Proof (CommMonoid M)) ->
    (p : Elem M) -> (u : Elem M) -> (hp : Proof (Irreducible p)) ->
    (hu : Proof (IsUnit u)) -> Proof (not (divide p u)) :=
  M => x => p => u => hp => hu =>
    mt (divide p u) (IsUnit p)
       (ifI (divide p u) (IsUnit p) (h => isUnit_of_dvd_unit M p u h hu))
       (Irreducible__not_isUnit M p hp).
\end{lstlisting}
Here \ttt{divide} and \ttt{not} are Informath's base constants, mapped from \ttt{Dvd.dvd} and
\ttt{Not}, and \ttt{ifI} is the natural deduction rule of implication introduction.
The formula reading of the statement is
$M \in \mathrm{Set}, \mathrm{CommMonoid}(M), p, u \in M, \mathrm{Irreducible}(p),
\mathrm{IsUnit}(u) \vdash \neg (p \mid u)$; its verbal reading is shown in
Section~\ref{sec:examples}.

\paragraph{Hidden arguments and coercions.} The first version of the exporter dropped implicit
arguments and erased coercions (casts between number types, the values of subtypes and of
bundled sets), as a reader does not see them in Mathlib. Both were mistakes, which the
comparison with lean2dk (Section~\ref{sec:lean2dk}) made clear. Dropping implicit arguments left
the types of the constants open, as \ttt{HVAdd\_\_hVAdd : (x1 : Elem X) -> (x2 : Elem Y) ->
Elem Z} with free $X$, $Y$, $Z$, and made declarations and uses disagree: \ttt{Equivalence.mk},
a constant whose type is a proposition and is therefore stated with its implicit arguments, was
applied without them, and the proof text read its arguments off the wrong positions, showing the
proofs of reflexivity and symmetry as the relation. Erasing coercions turned 45 statements into
tautologies: \ttt{AddSubgroup.coe\_sub}, $\uparrow(x - y) = \uparrow x - \uparrow y$ (subtraction
in a subgroup agrees with subtraction in the group), read ``$x - y$ is equal to $x - y$''.
Now the exporter keeps both, and writes what the readings hide as lines of a generated symbol
table:
\begin{lstlisting}
HVAdd__hVAdd : (X : Set) -> (Y : Set) -> (Z : Set) -> Elem X -> Elem Y -> Elem Z.
#HIDE HVAdd__hVAdd 1 2 3         -- the three types are implicit
#COERCION Subtype__val 3         -- the coercion reads as its third argument
\end{lstlisting}
Informath removes the hidden arguments before it looks up the entries, so that the hand-written
and generated entries count the explicit arguments as before, and a Mathlib update that moves an
implicit argument changes only the generated lines. A coercion is read as its argument, unless
that makes the two sides of an equation or an equivalence the same, and then it is shown:
``$\uparrow(x_1 - y)$ is equal to $\uparrow x_1 - \uparrow y$''. This leaves 11 tautological
readings (of 45) and the number of fully worded readings as it was.

\paragraph{Structures with names.} Dropping instance arguments loses a statement where it is
about two structures on one set: \ttt{TopologicalSpace.ext} says that two topologies with the
same open sets are equal, \ttt{IsOpen[f] = IsOpen[g] → f = g}, which with the instances
dropped read ``Let $X$ be a topological space such that $X$ is a topological space'', with a
hypothesis that says nothing. The exporter now translates a statement in two passes: the first
asks Lean's instance resolution, in the statement's context, for each instance argument, and
notes the types of those it would not find; the second keeps every instance argument of those
types, as a variant of the constant (\ttt{IsOpen\_\_in X f s}, ``$s$ is open in $f$''), makes
the variables in them elements of the set of the class's structures
(\ttt{(f : Elem (TopologicalSpace\_\_on X))}, ``let $f$ be a topology on $X$''), and states an
equality of such operations pointwise:
\reading{Let $X$ be a set. Let $f$ and $g$ be topologies on $X$ such that for all elements $x$
of $\wp X$, $x$ is open in $f$, if and only if $x$ is open in $g$. Then $f$ is equal to $g$.}
\noindent This changed 132 statements: the extensionality lemmas of orders, lattices, topologies
and distances, structures built by a definition (\ttt{t.induced f}, \ttt{Preorder.lift f}), and a
metric space's uniformity replaced by another ($\mathcal{U}_U = \mathcal{U}_m$).

\paragraph{2. Readings.} RunInformath translates the Dedukti declarations with the base table,
Mathlib's table (Section~\ref{sec:tables}) and the grammar, and gives a reading in each language
and a formula in \LaTeX. The index of all 9,126 statements is built in one call per mode, so
that the grammar (8 MB) is loaded once; in English, French and Czech this takes about eleven
minutes. German linearization turned out to be exponential in nested noun phrases (three levels
of ``the maximum of'' took 35 seconds), so German is read apart, with a time limit of one second
per statement, and it is not in the present index. A constant without an entry is shown as a
formula, $\mathrm{c}(x, y)$, when its arguments are formulas, rather than as ``$\mathrm{c}$
applied to $x$ and $y$''; this took the phrase ``applied to'' from about 5,400 to about 1,500
occurrences in the readings and proof texts. A function between sets given by expressions is
``a function from $I$ to $\wp X$'' (French ``de $I$ dans $\wp X$''), not ``a function from
elements of $I$ to elements of $\wp X$'' (1,726 statements), and a quantified variable with a
condition is one phrase, ``for all real numbers $\varepsilon > 0$'', or ``for all $\varepsilon > 0$
in $\mathbb{R}$'' where the set is a symbol (Section~\ref{sec:verbose}).

\paragraph{3. Term proofs.} A definiens that is a proof term is translated to a sequence of
proof units: each $\lambda$ becomes an assumption (``Let $n$ be an element of $\mathbb{N}$'',
``Assume that $p$ is prime''), each application of a lemma a conclusion, justified by the lemma.
Lean's logical constructors are mapped to natural deduction rules in Dedukti. The rewriting that
\ttt{simp} and \ttt{rw} leave in a proof term (\ttt{congrArg}, \ttt{Eq.mpr}, \ttt{of\_eq\_true})
is one step, ``By rewriting, \ldots'', whose margin names the simp lemmas used; the text itself
names no lemma, and the margin links each rule to its entry.

\paragraph{4. Tactic proofs.} Most of Mathlib's proofs are tactic scripts, whose text says
little: \ttt{rw [hn, mul\_one]}. Following the idea of Verbose Lean \cite{massot-2024}, we use
the goals instead. The exporter elaborates the module again with Lean's info trees, and records
for each tactic of a sequence the goal before it and the goals after it, translated to Dedukti.
A step then becomes units by comparing them: new hypotheses are assumed or stated, a closed
goal is a conclusion, a changed goal is ``it is enough to show that \ldots'', several goals are
cases. Each unit keeps the tactic as its justification:
\begin{lstlisting}
theorem Prime.eq_one_or_self_of_dvd {p : ℕ} (pp : p.Prime) (m : ℕ) (hm : m ∣ p) :
    m = 1 ∨ m = p := by
  obtain ⟨n, hn⟩ := hm
  have := pp.isUnit_or_isUnit hn
  rw [Nat.isUnit_iff, Nat.isUnit_iff] at this
  apply Or.imp_right _ this
  rintro rfl
  rw [hn, mul_one]
\end{lstlisting}
\begin{center}\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{rll}
1. & Let $n$ be an element of $\mathbb{N}$ such that $p = m \times n$. & \ttt{obtain ⟨n, hn⟩ := hm}\\
2. & $m$ is a unit or $n$ is a unit. & \ttt{have := pp.isUnit\_or\_isUnit hn}\\
3. & $m = 1$ or $n = 1$. & \ttt{rw [Nat.isUnit\_iff, Nat.isUnit\_iff] at this}\\
4. & It is enough to show that if $n = 1$, then $m = p$. & \ttt{apply Or.imp\_right \_ this}\\
5. & Assume that $n = 1$. & \ttt{rintro rfl}\\
6. & $m = p$. & \ttt{rw [hn, mul\_one]}
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The same units are read in the other languages: ``Stačí ukázat, že jestliže $n = 1$, pak
$m = p$'', ``Il suffit de montrer que \ldots''. A step that closes a goal or derives a fact says
how, where its tactic tells: ``By simplification'' (\ttt{simp}, 1,405 lines), ``By rewriting''
(\ttt{rw}, 1,335), ``By a routine argument'' (\ttt{grind}, \ttt{aesop}, 290), ``By definition''
(\ttt{rfl}, 74), ``By computation'' (\ttt{ring}, \ttt{omega}, 33); a step that applies a lemma
(\ttt{exact}, \ttt{apply}) is left as it is, as its tactic names the lemma. An additive theorem
that \ttt{to\_additive} makes from a multiplicative one has no source of its own, so it gets the
proof of its original, with each goal translated by \ttt{to\_additive}'s own table: 929 of the
1,118 such twins have a proof text.

\paragraph{Outputs.} The results are shown on web pages, one per part of Mathlib, where each
declaration has its Lean source, its Dedukti translation (folded), its readings and formula,
and its proof text \cite{mathlib-informath}. A stand-alone program, \ttt{mathlib-informath},
does the same for any Lean file or code on standard input, as plain text, HTML or PDF, in about
40 seconds, most of which Lean spends loading Mathlib.

For instance, the code on standard input
\begin{lstlisting}
theorem t (n : ℕ) (h : 0 < n) : n ≠ 0 := by omega
\end{lstlisting}
gives, with \ttt{-languages=Eng,Ger},
\begin{lstlisting}
t (theorem)
  English: Let $n$ be a natural number such that $0 < n$. Then $n \neq 0$.
  German: Sei $n$ eine natürliche Zahl derart, dass $0 < n$. Dann $n \neq 0$.
  Proof, English:
    1. $n \neq 0$.   [omega]
\end{lstlisting}
A user's own symbol table can be added on top of Mathlib's, for the concepts that the code
defines itself.

\paragraph{Folding proofs.} A long proof text is easier to read as an outline whose parts can be
opened one at a time. On the pages, the parts of a proof that correspond to subtrees of the proof
can be hidden and shown again, leaving the hypothesis and the conclusion. Two kinds of folds are
made from the lines and their justifications alone. The \emph{subproof} of an assumption is the
lines between it and the conclusion reached under it, as the scopes of \ttt{-proof-indent} show
them; a toggle at the assumption hides them. The \emph{derivation} of a line that cites earlier
lines (``\ttt{existsE} 45, 46, 53 (46)'', the parenthesized lines being discharged) is the lines
it depends on through its citations, transitively, except the assumptions still open at it and
the hypotheses of the theorem, which stay visible; a derivation that hides the same lines as a
subproof is left out. ``Fold all'' folds the subproofs and leaves the outline of the proof. The
folding is a small Python module of Informath (\ttt{scripts/proof\_folds.py}), used for the
proof texts of Mathlib, of Godement's book \cite{godement-index} and of the Edukera exercises
alike. The 7,658 proof texts have 1,596 subproof folds and 2,962 derivation folds.

\section{Reading versus checking: lean2dk}
\label{sec:lean2dk}

Our exporter is not the only translation of Lean to Dedukti. lean2dk
\cite{lean2dk}, described in Vaishnav's PhD thesis \cite{vaishnav-2026}, translates Lean so
that Dedukti can \emph{check} its proofs and pass them on to other systems. The two have
different purposes, and almost every difference follows from that.

lean2dk reads Lean as a functional pure type system and uses the encoding of Cousineau and
Dowek \cite{cousineau-dowek-2007}: a type is a code \ttt{t : Univ l} decoded by \ttt{El l t},
and a dependent function type is \ttt{Pi s1 s2 s3 A (x => B)}, with the universe of the
function type the \ttt{imax} of the others. Universe levels, with Lean's universe polymorphism
and its impredicative \ttt{Prop}, have an encoding of their own, a rewrite system that computes
normal forms of level expressions. Lean's definitional equalities become rewrite rules: the
unfolding of definitions, the reduction of recursors on constructors for every inductive type,
projections, structure eta, \ttt{Quot.lift}. Three of them, proof irrelevance, unit eta and
K-like reduction, cannot be rewrite rules, and are removed beforehand by Lean4Less, a modified
Lean kernel that translates a Lean environment into a smaller theory without them, inserting
casts where the original used them. Checking the result needs a patched Dedukti, and constants
whose checking would need infeasible computation (large \ttt{Nat} literals in unary form, the
exponential normal forms of \ttt{omega}'s machinery) are stubbed, that is, postulated.

Our exporter keeps none of this: types are \ttt{Set}, \ttt{Elem}, \ttt{Prop} and \ttt{Proof},
universes are collapsed, instance arguments are dropped (unless a statement gives one that instance
resolution would not find), there are no recursors or rewrite
rules, and constants are mapped to Informath's vocabulary where it has a word for them
(\ttt{HAdd.hAdd} to \ttt{plus}). Only proofs written as terms in the source are translated, and
tactic proofs are read from their goals. The difference shows on one theorem of Lean's library,
\ttt{Int.ofNat\_sub}: for natural numbers $m \leq n$, $\uparrow(n - m) = \uparrow n - \uparrow m$
in the integers. Our export is
\begin{lstlisting}
Int__ofNat_sub : (m : Elem Nat) -> (n : Elem Nat) -> (h : Proof (Leq m n)) ->
  Proof (Eq (Nat__cast Int (minus n m)) (minus (Nat__cast Int n) (Nat__cast Int m))).
\end{lstlisting}
and it has no proof, as the proof is a tactic. lean2dk's statement alone is about 1,300
characters, as it keeps \ttt{Nat.cast} with the instance \ttt{instNatCastInt}, \ttt{HSub.hSub}
with its three types, levels and instance, and \ttt{El} and \ttt{Pi} at every binder; the proof is
another 14,600 characters, and translating this one theorem brings in 430 constants, 700 KB
of Dedukti in nine files, with 14 constants stubbed. The thesis reports output two to three
orders of magnitude larger than Lean's own \ttt{.olean} files, and the translation of only
lower-level parts of the standard library to the end \cite[Section~7.1]{vaishnav-2026}.

Comparing the two showed two flaws of ours, which Section~\ref{sec:pipeline} describes as fixed:
before, our export of \ttt{Int.ofNat\_sub} was \ttt{Eq (minus n m) (minus n m)}, a tautology,
since the casts were erased; and our signatures had free type variables where implicit
arguments had been dropped. lean2dk never identifies two things, so it cannot lose a statement
this way. What we still leave out is what a checker needs and a reader does not: universes,
instances and computation. The last of these has a cost for us too: most of the 45 proofs that
get no text are ones where Lean accepts a $\lambda$ as the proof of a proposition that is a
$\forall$ only after a definition, such as \ttt{upperBounds}, is unfolded, which lean2dk's
rewrite rules would do. The two translations are complementary: one gives a reading, the other a
check, of the same declaration.

\section{Writing versus reading: Verbose Lean}
\label{sec:verbose}

Verbose Lean \cite{massot-2024} works in the other direction. Its controlled natural language
is Lean \emph{input}: a student writes a proof sentence by sentence, each sentence one tactic,
and Lean checks it. The language is a set of Lean syntax rules, about 56 phrase templates in
English (``Fix \ldots'', ``By \ldots\ applied to \ldots\ using that \ldots\ we get \ldots\ such
that \ldots'', ``Let's prove that \ldots\ works'', ``It suffices to prove \ldots'', ``We conclude
by \ldots''), with Lean terms in the slots. Formulas are not put into words, on purpose: the
pedagogy is to keep stating, proving and using a fact apart, so ``Fix $\varepsilon > 0$'' is not
the statement $\forall \varepsilon > 0$, ``we get $\delta$ such that'' takes a witness rather than
claiming that one exists, and $\Rightarrow$ appears only in statements. There is no
morphology; French is a hand-made copy of the syntax files. Massot doubts that a controlled
language is an efficient input format for mathematicians, and expects formal mathematics to
reach its readers ``by translating formalized mathematics to natural language a posteriori'',
which is our direction.

The first example of Massot's paper shows both. Its plain tactic proof that continuity implies
sequential continuity,
\begin{lstlisting}
intro ε ε_pos
rcases hf ε ε_pos with ⟨δ, δ_pos, Hf⟩
rcases hu δ δ_pos with ⟨N, Hu⟩
use N
intro n n_ge
exact Hf (u n) (Hu n n_ge)
\end{lstlisting}
is written by a student in Verbose Lean as
\begin{lstlisting}
Fix ε > 0
By hf applied to ε using that ε > 0 we get δ such that
  δ_pos : δ > 0 and Hf : ∀ x, |x - x₀| ≤ δ ⇒ |f x - f x₀| ≤ ε
By hu applied to δ using that δ > 0 we get N such that Hu : ∀ n ≥ N, |u n - x₀| ≤ δ
Let's prove that N works : ∀ n ≥ N, |f (u n) - f x₀| ≤ ε
Fix n ≥ N
By Hf applied to u n it suffices to prove |u n - x₀| ≤ δ
We conclude by Hu applied to n using that n ≥ N
\end{lstlisting}
and read by our pipeline, from the plain proof and with Verbose's two definitions in a user's
table, as
\begin{center}\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{rll}
1. & Let $\varepsilon \in \mathbb{R}$ such that $\varepsilon > 0$. & \ttt{intro ε ε\_pos}\\
2. & Let $\delta \in \mathbb{R}$ such that $\delta > 0$ and for all elements $x_2$ of
     $\mathbb{R}$, & \\
   & \quad if $|x_2 - x_0| \leq \delta$, then $|f(x_2) - f(x_0)| \leq \varepsilon$. &
     \ttt{rcases hf ε ε\_pos with \ldots}\\
3. & Let $N \in \mathbb{N}$ such that for all $n \geq N$ in $\mathbb{N}$,
     $|u(n) - x_0| \leq \delta$. & \ttt{rcases hu δ δ\_pos with \ldots}\\
4. & It is enough to show that for all $n_1 \geq N$ in $\mathbb{N}$, & \\
   & \quad $|f(u(n_1)) - f(x_0)| \leq \varepsilon$. & \ttt{use N}\\
5. & \quad Let $n \in \mathbb{N}$ such that $n \geq N$. & \ttt{intro n n\_ge}\\
6. & \quad $|f(u(n)) - f(x_0)| \leq \varepsilon$. & \ttt{exact Hf (u n) (Hu n n\_ge)}
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The structure is the same, one sentence per tactic: our reading of a tactic proof is the inverse
of Verbose Lean. Verbose reads better as a paper proof: ``we get $\delta$ such that'', ``$N$
works'', ``it suffices''. The comparison also showed defects of ours, which are now fixed:
$\varepsilon$, $\delta$ and $x_0$ were renamed $x$, $W$, $x_2$, as the exporter kept only ASCII
names; $(f \circ u)\, n$ lost its argument; and $\forall \varepsilon > 0$ was ``for all real
numbers $x$, if $x > 0$, then'', as Informath had no bounded quantifiers. It has them now, with the
kind kept, as ``there exists $q \neq 0$'' would not say that $q$ is an integer: ``for all real
numbers $\varepsilon > 0$'', ``pour tous les nombres réels $\varepsilon > 0$''; where the kind is a
symbol, as $\mathbb{R}$ on the pages, ``for all $\varepsilon > 0$ in $\mathbb{R}$'', ``pour tout
$\varepsilon > 0$ dans $\mathbb{R}$''. The same term proof written with pattern matching,
\ttt{fun δ ⟨δ\_pos, Hf⟩ => \ldots}, had no proof text, and now has one, as the exporter translates
matching on $\wedge$ and $\exists$ to their elimination rules.

The two can also be combined. Since our pipeline already pairs each tactic with a sentence, the
sentence can be Verbose's: a Lean command, \ttt{\#verbalize}, elaborates a theorem with an
ordinary tactic proof and writes each step as Verbose Lean, the formulas printed by Lean with
Verbose's notations. For the proof above it gives
\begin{lstlisting}
Fix ε > 0
By hf applied to ε using ε_pos we get δ such that (δ_pos : δ > 0) and
  (Hf : ∀ (x : ℝ), |x - x₀| ≤ δ ⇒ |f x - f x₀| ≤ ε)
By hu applied to δ using δ_pos we get N such that (Hu : ∀ n ≥ N, |u n - x₀| ≤ δ)
Let's prove that N works
Fix n ≥ N
We conclude by Hf applied to u n using Hu n n_ge
\end{lstlisting}
which is Massot's own text but for the named variant ``using \ttt{ε\_pos}'', and which Lean with
Verbose checks again: a round trip, and the only informalization in this paper whose text is
checked. The prototype covers \ttt{intro}, \ttt{rcases}/\ttt{obtain}, \ttt{use} and \ttt{exact},
leaving other tactics as they are, which Verbose allows. In the other direction, Verbose's tested
phrases could become the proof lines of Informath in all its languages, ``Fix $\varepsilon > 0$''
for ``Let $\varepsilon \in \mathbb{R}$ such that $\varepsilon > 0$''.

\section{Inferring the symbol tables}
\label{sec:tables}

Informath's own lexicon covers basic mathematics; Mathlib has tens of thousands of concepts.
The first tables were written by hand: 108 entries for the constants that our first modules use,
with suggestions from docstrings and names checked by the grammar. This does not scale, so we
infer a table for all of Mathlib's definitions.

\paragraph{The definitions.} \ttt{export --definitions} imports all of Mathlib (20 seconds) and
exports every declaration that may need a wording: definitions, structures, classes, fields,
inductive types, but not theorems, instances, tactics, notations, auxiliary or deprecated
declarations. Of 56,852 such constants, 38,667 remain after filtering, each with its Dedukti
type, explicit argument names and docstring. Its type tells its category: a function or constant
(\ttt{Exp}), a type (\ttt{Kind}), or a predicate (\ttt{Prop}).

\paragraph{Candidates.} For each definition, candidate wordings are made in two ways.
\emph{From the docstring}, patterns pick noun phrases and predicates: ``\ttt{`f n`} is the X of
\ttt{`n`}'', ``The X of \ldots\ is'', ``A set is X if'', with the backticked argument names
replaced by their positions \ttt{\#1}, \ttt{\#2}. \emph{From the name}, the last component is
split at capitals and underscores, and Mathlib's abbreviations are spelled out (\ttt{minFac}:
minimum factor; \ttt{card}: cardinality; \ttt{AddMonoidHom}: additive monoid homomorphism,
with \emph{Add} as a modifier rather than ``sum''). The name gives ``the X of \ttt{\#1}'' for a
function, ``X of \ttt{\#1}'' for a type, ``\ttt{\#1} is (a) X'' for a predicate
(\ttt{IsCountablyCompact}: ``\ttt{\#1} is countably compact''), ``\ttt{\#1} has X'' for one
named after a verb (\ttt{HasLimits}, \ttt{PreservesFiniteLimits}). A few conventions needed
special rules: a constructor \ttt{Bicone.ofLimitCone} is named after its namespace (``the bicone
of \ttt{\#1}''), a function \ttt{pointOfClosedPoint} after its head, but a type
\ttt{PartitionOfUnity} keeps its ``of''.

For example, \ttt{Ordinal.cof : Ordinal → Cardinal} has the docstring ``The cofinality on an
ordinal is the \ttt{Order.cof} of any isomorphic linear order''. Its first pattern gives
``the cofinality on an ordinal of \ttt{\#1}'', which does not parse; with the generic
prepositional phrase cut out, ``the cofinality of \ttt{\#1}'' does, and it is also what the name
gives, as \ttt{cof} is spelled out as \emph{cofinality}. Since the candidate shares a word with
the name, it is accepted; a docstring wording that shares no word with the name, such as ``the
ring'' for \ttt{HahnSeries.toPowerSeries}, is not tried.

\paragraph{Checking.} All 41,000 candidates are parsed by the grammar in one call (30 seconds).
A candidate is accepted if its parse has the category and the arity of the constant; the first
one accepted becomes the entry. Many constants take explicit parameters before their main
arguments (a category, a Grothendieck topology); a wording may leave them out, as \ttt{Eqt}
above leaves out the type. But leaving out the wrong argument gives a false reading:
\ttt{IsRelUpperSet s P} was first read ``$P$ is a relation upper set''. A predicate of two
objects is therefore read as binary, ``\ttt{\#1} is a relative upper set with respect to
\ttt{\#2}'', with the subject chosen by the docstring (the argument that it does \emph{not} name
after ``relative to'', ``for'', ``over''), and without such a clue it gets no reading. We prefer
a symbolic reading to a wrong one.

\paragraph{The lexicon.} Candidates fail mostly because of unknown words. These are added to the
grammar: a word that recurs in the candidates gets a noun, adjective, adverb or verb entry, with
its category and plural from the RGL's English dictionary (\ttt{DictEng}), and a word that the
dictionary lacks is admitted if three docstrings use it. Junk had to be filtered out by hand-made
lists: docstrings contain function words that the dictionary lacks (\emph{because},
\emph{without}) and Lean jargon (\emph{simproc}, \emph{defeq}). The result is 507 English words,
from \emph{cocone}, \emph{equicontinuous} and \emph{hypercover} to \emph{Zariski}.

For the other languages, we first tried GF's WordNet \cite{miller-1995,angelov-2020}, choosing
senses by their domain tags: only 117 of 789 words were found, 13 of them in a sense tagged
\emph{mathematics}, and even those were mixed (\emph{hexagon}: \emph{Sechseck}, correct;
\emph{minus}: \emph{Weg}, wrong; \emph{triplet}: \emph{trois}, wrong). Mathlib's jargon is not
there. We therefore keep the translations in a table, one line per word with its French,
German, Swedish and Czech forms (gender, plural), written by Claude and to be reviewed, with an
empty cell where unsure; the grammar modules are generated from it. Where a language lacks a
word, the index keeps the reading made without the generated table, which shows the constant
symbolically.

\paragraph{The lexicon, a second time.} Most definitions left without an entry have a word in
their name that the grammar lacks as a noun or adjective, although its lexicon (4,560 words) has
a word for it. A second round replaces such words: a verb by its nominalization, after its object
(\ttt{restrictScalars}: ``the scalar restriction of \ttt{\#1}''); an abbreviation by the word of
the lexicon that it abbreviates and that the docstrings of the definitions named with it use
most (\ttt{denom}: denominator, \ttt{coeq}: coequalizer); a proper name by its spelling
(\ttt{kronecker}: Kronecker); and \ttt{desc} and \ttt{forget} by ``the induced morphism'' and
``the forgetful functor''. As long names read word by word were mostly wrong, the round is
restricted to names of at most two words. It gives 887 entries with 135 replacements, which are
listed for review.

\paragraph{Notation.} The generated entries were verbal only, although Mathlib has notation for
many of its concepts. The exporter therefore prints each definition applied to variables for its
explicit arguments with Lean's own delaborator, with the scoped notations of its namespaces
opened (\ttt{n !} is scoped in \ttt{Nat}). Where the result is not the name applied to the
arguments, it is notation, and it is written in \LaTeX\ token by token, the variables becoming
the argument positions:
\begin{lstlisting}
Int__floor : "the floor of #1" | $\lfloor #1 \rfloor$
RingEquiv  : "ring isomorphism of #1 and #2" | $#1 \simeq_{+*} #2$
NNReal     : $\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$       -- no wording: the notation alone
\end{lstlisting}
A run of symbols is one token, so that Lean's \ttt{M $\simeq$+* N} and \ttt{$\mathbb{R}$$\geq$0}
get their own \LaTeX, and a token without one rules the notation out, as do binders
(\ttt{$\sum$ x $\in$ s, f x}) and Lean's bracketed subscripts (\ttt{M $\otimes$[R] N},
\ttt{f\^{}[n]}), which \LaTeX\ writes otherwise. This
first gave 42 definitions a symbolic variant, among them $\bot$, $\top$, $s^c$, $|x|$, $\|x\|$,
$\lceil x \rceil$, $s \mathbin{\Delta} t$, $\sqrt{x}$, $A^T$ and $\# s$. Logging the tokens that
had no \LaTeX\ showed what was missing: with some sixty more (Lean's $+_v$, $\to_{*}$,
${}^{\times}$, $\bigcup_0$, Greek letters), 115 definitions have one, among them $x +_v s$, $M^{\times}$,
$M \to_{*} N$, $R \to_{+*} S$, $\bigcup S$ and $\pi$. The variant matters beyond the
definitions: an equation is written as a formula only if all its constants have a symbolic form,
so that the cosets $x +_v s$ of a subgroup were read ``the sum of $x$ and $s$'' before. The
number is still small because the most common notations ($+$, $\mid$, $\leq$, $n!$) belong to
constants that are mapped to Informath's own, and many others are binders. (The definition
pages of Section~\ref{sec:allread} were read with the 42.)

\section{Structures and inheritance}
\label{sec:structures}

Much of Mathlib's mathematics lives in its \emph{classes}: \ttt{Monoid}, \ttt{Group},
\ttt{Ring}, \ttt{Field}, \ttt{Lattice}, \ttt{Module}, \ttt{TopologicalSpace}, some 2,000 in
all. A class is a structure, a record of data and laws over a carrier type, and it is
\emph{extended} by other classes, often by several at once:
\begin{lstlisting}
/-- A `Monoid` is a `Semigroup` with an element `1` such that `1 * a = a * 1 = a`. -/
class Monoid (M : Type*) extends One M, Mul M, Semigroup M, MulOneClass M, NPow M where
  npow := npowRecAuto
  protected npow_zero (x : M) : x ^ 0 = 1 := by intros; rfl
  protected npow_succ (n : ℕ) (x : M) : x ^ (n + 1) = x ^ n * x := by intros; rfl

class Group (G : Type*) extends DivInvMonoid G where
  protected inv_mul_cancel : ∀ a : G, a⁻¹ * a = 1
\end{lstlisting}
Statements use the classes as predicates of their carriers: \ttt{[Group G]} becomes ``$G$ is a
group''. But what a group \emph{is} is spread over the hierarchy, and Lean's way of putting it
together is not a textbook's. Lean flattens: an instance of \ttt{Monoid} has all the fields of all
its ancestors, and the ancestors include classes that exist only to carry notation (\ttt{One},
\ttt{Mul}), technical classes that fix the implementation (\ttt{NPow}, the power $x^n$ with a
default definition, and \ttt{DivInvMonoid}, a monoid with an inverse and a division but no
laws), and the additive copy of every multiplicative class, generated by the attribute
\ttt{to\_additive}. A textbook names one parent and lists what is new: \emph{a monoid is a
semigroup with an identity element; a group is a monoid in which every element has an
inverse; a commutative group is a group in which $ab = ba$}.

\paragraph{Four options.} The design started from a note of options, written by Claude at the
first author's request, each option with samples read by Informath. (A) \emph{Flattened}: each class a structure with all its fields; complete and
correct, but a commutative group repeats the monoid and the group. (B) \emph{The parent as a
clause}: the structure has its carrier, its parent's predicate as a hypothesis, as Lean's
instance argument, and only its own fields. (C) \emph{An extension judgement}: B's encoding,
read by a new GF judgement as a definition by extension. (D) \emph{The class as a defined
predicate}: ``$G$ is a group if $G$ is a monoid and there is a function $i$ such that \ldots'',
its data quantified existentially. D unifies the class with the predicate that statements
use, and fits proofs: a use of the field \ttt{mul\_comm} becomes an elimination of the
definition, ``since $G$ is a commutative group, $ab = ba$''; it loses that the identity and the
inverse are data chosen by the structure. We implemented B and C for the structure and D for
the predicate, and show both.

\paragraph{The export.} For a class $C$ with carrier $G$ and primary parent $P$, the exporter
writes a structure and a definition:
\begin{lstlisting}
Monoid__structure : (M : Set) -> (h : Proof (Semigroup M)) -> ({|1|} : Elem M) ->
  (one_mul : Proof (forall M (a => Eqt M (times {|1|} a) a))) ->
  (mul_one : Proof (forall M (a => Eqt M (times a {|1|}) a))) -> Structure.
def Monoid : (M : Set) -> Prop := M => and (Semigroup M)
  (exists M (e => and (forall M (a => Eqt M (times e a) a)) (forall M (a => Eqt M (times a e) a)))).
\end{lstlisting}
Several choices make this read like mathematics:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{The primary parent} is the one the docstring names, as Mathlib writes its docstrings
  as a textbook would (``A \ttt{Monoid} is a \ttt{Semigroup} with \ldots'', ``A \ttt{Group} is a
  \ttt{Monoid} with an operation ${}^{-1}$ \ldots''), skipping \ttt{MulOneClass} and
  \ttt{DivInvMonoid}; without one, the ancestor with the most fields. The fields of the parent
  are its own: the structure lists only the fields that are new with respect to it.
\item \emph{Fields are named by their notation}: the identity is a variable $1$ (written
  \ttt{\{|1|\}} in Dedukti), the inverse ${}^{-1}$, the order $\leq$. A field has a type by its
  shape: a binary operation \ttt{Oper G}, a unary one \ttt{UOper G}, written $a^{-1}$ or $-a$, a
  relation \ttt{Rel G}, an action \ttt{Action R M} of a scalar on the carrier. The parent's
  operations are written as statements write them ($a \times b$, $a \leq b$).
\item \emph{Technical fields are left out}: a field with a default value (\ttt{npow},
  \ttt{zpow}, \ttt{div}), data not built of the carrier (\ttt{natCast}, \ttt{nnqsmul}), and
  the laws that mention them. They are listed on the page.
\item \emph{Parameters}: a class over another type, as \ttt{Module R M}, has its scalars and
  their instance (\ttt{[Semiring R]}) as clauses before the carrier, which are read as a
  hypothesis. Greek carriers ($\alpha$) get a textbook letter ($R$ for a ring, $K$ for a field,
  $M$ for a monoid or a module), and out-parameters ($\alpha, \beta$ of a class of morphisms
  \ttt{F}) are parameters, \ttt{F} being the carrier.
\item \emph{Additive classes} are derived from the multiplicative ones by \ttt{to\_additive}'s
  own translation table, which maps the names of the class, its parent and its fields, and the
  notation ($*$ to $+$, $1$ to $0$, $a^{-1}$ to $-a$).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The grammar.} Informath had a \ttt{StructureJmt}, which reads a structure as a list of
clauses (``a monoid is an algebraic structure, where $M$ is a set, $M$ is a semigroup,
$1$ is an element of $M$, \ldots''). We added judgements of definition by extension to its
abstract syntax:
\begin{lstlisting}
ExtensionJmt      : Label -> Kind -> Kind -> Ident -> [ArgKind] -> Prop -> Jmt
ExtensionHyposJmt : Label -> [Hypo] -> Kind -> Kind -> Ident -> [ArgKind] -> Prop -> Jmt
\end{lstlisting}
with the defined kind, the parent kind, the carrier, the new data and their laws (and variants
without data or without laws). They are produced by an NLG rule from a \ttt{StructureJmt} whose
carrier is followed by its parent read as a noun (from the parent's entry, ``\ttt{\#1} is a
semigroup''); the clauses before the carrier become hypotheses. The concrete syntax is written
once with the RGL, as ``a $K$ is a $P$ $x$ with $D$ such that $Q$'', and the semantics maps
the judgements back to \ttt{StructureJmt}, so that the round trip to Dedukti is preserved.
Aggregation of quantifiers inside conjunctions (``for all elements $a$ and $b$ of $G$'',
``for all $a$, $P$ and $Q$'') makes the laws shorter. The structures of rings, orders, lattices
and modules then read:
\reading{A group is a monoid $G$ with an operation ${}^{-1}$ on $G$ such that for all elements
$a$ of $G$, $a^{-1} \times a = 1$.\\
A ring is a semiring $R$ with an operation $-$ on $R$ such that for all elements $a$ of $R$,
$-a + a = 0$.\\
A distributive lattice is a lattice $G$ such that for all elements $x$, $y$ and $z$ of $G$,
$(x \sqcup y) \sqcap (x \sqcup z) \leq x \sqcup (y \sqcap z)$.\\
Let $R$ be a semiring. Then a module over $R$ is an additive commutative monoid $M$ with an
action $\bullet$ of $R$ on $M$ such that for all elements $x$ and $y$ of $R$, for all elements
$b$ of $M$, $(x \times y) \bullet b = x \bullet (y \bullet b)$, \ldots\\
Un groupe est un monoïde $G$ avec une opération ${}^{-1}$ sur $G$ tel que pour tous les
éléments $a$ de $G$, $a^{-1} \times a = 1$.}
\noindent and the predicates:
\reading{Let $M$ be a set. Then $M$ is a monoid, if $M$ is a semigroup and there exists an
element $e$ of $M$, such that for all elements $a$ of $M$, $e \times a = a$ and $a \times e =
a$.}
\noindent Two pages of the index show 50 structures and predicates, from magmas to fields, from
preorders to distributive lattices, and modules, in all five languages; 50 of 50 are fully
worded in English.

\paragraph{All the classes.} The same export runs over all of Mathlib's classes, with all classes
as possible parents, for the definition pages (Section~\ref{sec:allread}). 1,889 of the 2,009
classes export as structures. On the pages, a class worded as a noun is read as its structure,
with its predicate under it; any other class as its predicate (``$G$ is unique, if
$\mathrm{Inhabited}$ holds for $G$ and for all elements $a$ of $G$, $a =
\mathrm{Inhabited\_\_default}$'');
a class whose fields are all technical, such as \ttt{TopologicalSpace} with its \ttt{IsOpen},
keeps its declaration. Of the 1,948 classes on the pages, 359 read as extensions (``a metric
space is a pseudo metric space $G$ such that for all elements $x$ and $y$ of $G$, if the
distance of $x$ and $y$ is equal to $0$, then $x = y$''), 510 as defined predicates, and 86 as
lists of clauses, as their parent is an adjective (\ttt{IsDomain}: ``$G$ is cancellative'') or
has two arguments (\ttt{Module R M} by \ttt{DistribMulAction R M}). 877 have no fields of their
own: they combine parents (\ttt{LeftCancelSemigroup extends Semigroup, IsLeftCancelMul}), and
the parents other than the primary one are not yet clauses, so these keep the plain reading.

compiler (a list category whose concrete functions grew to $5 \times 10^{13}$), the reason why
size-optimized grammars broke all proof texts (the proof units were not reachable from the start
category), and the decision to keep the translations reviewable. Others were the agent's:
the iteration over hundreds of failing cases, measuring each change on the whole of Mathlib, and
testing a resource (WordNet) before relying on it. The last rounds show the pattern on a small

to lose information (``there exists $q \neq 0$''), and the first author then designed the
function kinds himself (\ttt{FunExpKind}, with \emph{dans} in French). All the words written by
Claude, in all
languages, are to be reviewed by native speakers.

\section{Examples}
\label{sec:examples}

\paragraph{Definitions.} Mathlib defines a prime number as an irreducible element:
\begin{lstlisting}
def Prime (p : ℕ) := Irreducible p
\end{lstlisting}
\reading{Let $p \in \mathbb{N}$. Then $p$ is prime, if $p$ is irreducible.\\
Soit $p \in \mathbb{N}$. Alors $p$ est premier si $p$ est irréductible.\\
Låt $p \in \mathbb{N}$. Då är $p$ primt, om $p$ är irreducibelt.}
\noindent Informath prefers the symbolic declaration of a variable of a set, ``Let $p \in
\mathbb{N}$'', to the verbal ``Let $p$ be a natural number'', also with a condition: ``Let $p,
u \in M$ such that \ldots''.
\noindent Wordings from the generated table, with the arguments shown by their names:
``the cofinality of $o$'', ``the normalizer of $H$'', ``$C$ has limits'', ``$F$ preserves
finite limits'', ``$P$ is separated with respect to $J$'', ``$M$ is a perfect matching''.

\paragraph{Theorems.} Euclid's theorem in five languages:
\begin{lstlisting}
theorem exists_infinite_primes (n : ℕ) : ∃ p, n ≤ p ∧ Prime p
\end{lstlisting}
\reading{Let $n \in \mathbb{N}$. Then there exists a natural number $p$, such that
$n \leq p$ and $p$ is prime.\\
Soit $n \in \mathbb{N}$. Alors il existe un nombre naturel $p$ tel que $n \leq p$ et $p$ soit
premier.\\
Sei $n \in \mathbb{N}$. Dann gibt es eine natürliche Zahl $p$, derart, dass $n \leq p$
und $p$ prim ist.\\
Låt $n \in \mathbb{N}$. Då finns det ett naturligt tal $p$, så att $n \leq p$ och $p$
är primt.\\
Nechť $n \in \mathbb{N}$. Tedy existuje přirozené číslo $p$, \textcolor{red}{takový}, že
$n \leq p$ a $p$ je prvočíselné.}
\noindent The aggregation of hypotheses makes statements with many variables readable:
\reading{Let $M$ be a commutative monoid. Let $p, u \in M$ such that $p$ is irreducible and
$u$ is a unit. Then $p$ does not divide $u$.}
\noindent (\ttt{Irreducible.not\_dvd\_isUnit}; the Lean statement has the implicit \ttt{M},
\ttt{p}, \ttt{u} and the instance \ttt{[CommMonoid M]}.)

\paragraph{Term proofs.} The definiens of \ttt{exists\_infinite\_primes}, with its local
definitions and \ttt{have}s, becomes a proof text of fourteen lines, which starts:
\reading{1. To prove that if $\mathrm{minFac}(n! + 1) \leq n$, then we have a contradiction,
assume that $\mathrm{minFac}(n! + 1) \leq n$.\quad 2. $0 < n!$.\quad 3. The successor of
$0$ is less than the successor of $n!$.\quad 4. $n! + 1 \neq 1$.\quad 5.
$\mathrm{minFac}(n! + 1)$ is prime.\quad \ldots}
\noindent Each line is justified by the lemma it applies (\ttt{Nat.factorial\_pos},
\ttt{Nat.succ\_lt\_succ}, \ldots), linked to its own entry. The proof that the relation of
having the same left coset, $x +_v s = y +_v s$, is an equivalence,
\begin{lstlisting}
@Equivalence.mk _ (LeftCosetEquivalence s) (fun _ => rfl) Eq.symm Eq.trans
\end{lstlisting}
becomes sixteen lines, in three parts for reflexivity, symmetry and transitivity, of which the
first is
\reading{1. To prove that for all elements $x_1$ of $X$, $x_1 +_v s = x_1 +_v s$, let
$x_1 \in X$.\quad 2. $x_1 +_v s = x_1 +_v s$. \ttt{[rfl]}\quad 3. For all elements $x_1$ of $X$,
$x_1 +_v s = x_1 +_v s$. \ttt{[forallI 1, 2]}}
\noindent and the last ``$\mathrm{LeftAddCosetEquivalence}(s)$ is an equivalence relation''
\ttt{[Equivalence.mk 3, 8, 15]}, in Czech ``je relace ekvivalence''.

The longest proof text, of \ttt{IsCompact.elim\_directed\_cover} (an open directed cover of a
compact set has a single member that includes it), has 58 lines, nested seven scopes deep.
With all subproofs folded, it shows its outline:
\reading{1. Assume that $I$ is nonempty, $s$ is compact, for all elements $i_2$ of $I$, $U(i_2)$ is open, \ldots\
and $U$ is directed.\quad 2. To prove that for all elements $i_0$ of $I$, there exists an
element $i_{47}$ of $I$ such that $s \subseteq U(i_{47})$, \ldots\ \emph{(lines 3--55
folded)}\quad 56. There exists an element $i_1$ of $I$ such that $s \subseteq U(i_1)$.
\ttt{[IsCompact.induction\_on 1, 4, 16, 38, 55]}\quad 57. \ldots\quad 58. There exists an
element $i$ of $I$ such that $s \subseteq U(i)$. \ttt{[Nonempty.elim 1, 57]}}
\noindent Opening line 2 shows the four cases of the induction on compact sets (the empty set, a
subset, a union, a neighbourhood of each point), each folded again. Reading this proof showed a
bug: the instance \ttt{[hι : Nonempty ι]}, which the exporter leaves out of statements as a
technical class, is used by the proof (\ttt{Nonempty.elim hι}), which then referred to a
variable that nothing bound, shown as a line ``Assume that $I$ is nonempty'' justified by
\ttt{hiota}. Now such an instance of \ttt{Nonempty}, \ttt{Inhabited}, \ttt{Nontrivial} or
\ttt{CharZero}, classes that a textbook does state, is kept where the proof uses it (11
statements), and the statement now begins ``Let $X$ be a topological space. Let $s \in \wp X$. Let
$I$ be a nonempty set.''

\paragraph{Bad cases.} Not everything reads well:
\begin{itemize}
\item a constant without a wording stays symbolic: ``Let $n$ be a natural number such that
  $\mathrm{Coprime}(2, n)$'' in French, where the English has ``$2$ and $n$ are coprime'';
\item mismatched agreement: Czech \emph{takový} above, which should be \emph{takové}; ``Let
  $s$ be \textcolor{red}{a} upper set'';
\item literal wordings: a table entry given as a string is parsed word by word, so that ``is
  equal to'' became Czech ``je rovná \textcolor{red}{do}'' (\emph{rovná} takes the dative) until
  the entry was written as the grammar's lexical \ttt{equalset\_Adj2};
\item generated wordings that say little: \ttt{BoundedOrderHom.dual}, a bundled map without
  explicit arguments, is ``the dual'';
\item German: ``Dann $2 \leq p$'' without a verb, and 51 statements over the time limit.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{All the definitions in English.}
\label{sec:allread}
Finally, all 38,667 definitions were read in English and as formulas, and then in French, as the statements on the
pages are: each definition's statement, with its definiens where it has one (25,491 of the
exported constants have one that the exporter translates), and each class as its structure or
predicate (Section~\ref{sec:structures}), by the full symbol table. With eight
RunInformath processes and a time limit of one second per definition and mode, this took about
an hour on a laptop, and those over the limit were read again on a quiet machine. 38,517
definitions (99.6\%) got a reading, 119 went over the time limit, and 31 got none; 38,546 got a
formula. About 12,000 readings (31\%) show no constant symbolically; the others show the
constants that have no wording, often from the definiens, as operators. (The share was 33\%
before the classes were read as structures: a class's fields bring in more constants than its
bare declaration, ``we can say that $M$ is a monoid''.) The readings are published on 111
static pages of at most 400 definitions, by part of Mathlib, with a search page over all of
them. For example, \ttt{Quiver.IsThin}:
\reading{Let $V$ be a set. Then $V$ is thin, if for all elements $a$ and $b$ of $V$, the set of
homomorphisms of $a$ and $b$ is a subsingleton.\\[1mm]
$V \in \mathrm{Set} \vdash \operatorname{IsThin}(V) :\Leftrightarrow \forall a \in V .\ \forall b \in V .\
\mathrm{Subsingleton}(\operatorname{Hom}(a, b))$}

wordings into GF trees and linearizes them in French; the French rows are hidden on the pages
until asked for, as on the other pages. The French reading of \ttt{Quiver.IsThin} is
\reading{Soit $V$ un ensemble. Alors $V$ est fin si pour tous les éléments $a$ et $b$ de $V$,
l'ensemble d'homomorphismes de $a$ et $b$ est un sous-singleton.}
\noindent where \emph{fin} for \emph{thin} is a word to be reviewed. In the first run, 9,774 of the
French readings (26\%) used a word that had no French form: 263 words of Informath's lexicon,

led by \emph{opposite} (in 1,554 readings), \emph{lift} (1,053) and \emph{carrier} (950).

\section{Known problems and open issues}
\label{sec:problems}

\paragraph{Coverage.} All of Mathlib's definitions are read, but only in English, as formulas
and in French, and its
theorems only in part: the 9,126 statements of the pages are a small part of its 194,000
theorems, which the same pipeline could read, with their proofs, but which we have not run on;
German and Swedish are not in the present index. Of the
definitions, 119 go over the time limit in English and 243 in French, and two thirds of the readings still show some constant
symbolically, mostly constants of the definientia that have no wording. The generated table is
unreviewed, and in our samples 18 of 60 of its first wordings (30\%) and 6 of 60 of those made with
the lexicon (10\%) are wrong: composite names
(\ttt{quotientIdealMapEquivTensorQuot}) become nonsense when read word by word; bundled maps
without arguments get bare readings; about 2,500 unknown words are abbreviations of jargon that
we do not spell out. Long \ttt{simp} goals make poor reading, and limits and filters are read
only as formulas (``$\mathrm{Tendsto}(f, l_1, l_2)$'' for ``$f$ tends to \ldots'').

\paragraph{Structures.} Multiple inheritance is reduced to one parent: the other parents of a
class (\ttt{IsLeftCancelMul} of \ttt{LeftCancelSemigroup}) should be clauses, ``a left
cancellative semigroup is a semigroup that is left cancellative''; 877 classes wait for this.
A parent that is an adjective (``cancellative'') or that has two arguments
(\ttt{DistribMulAction R M}) gives a list of clauses rather than an extension. D', the
informal form of D (``for every $a$ there is a $b$ such that $ba = 1$'' rather than ``there is
a function $i$''), is not generated, and the parent's multiplication is written $a \times b$
where a textbook writes $ab$.

\paragraph{Language.} Agreement of ``such that'' with an existential's kind fails in Czech and
French; multi-letter variables (\ttt{self}, \ttt{motive}) are shown as constants; a lemma used
unapplied in a proof term gives garbled text, as does a coercion used unapplied
($\uparrow$ alone); a binder over a proof (``for all elements $h$ of $\mathrm{Proof}$ applied to
\ldots'') should read ``if \ldots''. About 1,500 occurrences of
``applied to'' remain where an
argument is itself in words, and 11 readings are still tautologies. The French and Czech words
added for the definitions and the theorems, and the readings themselves, are not checked. ``An unary operation'': the RGL's English
article takes \emph{an} before every word beginning with \emph{un}. The words of four languages need native review,
and German linearization needs a fix in the RGL.

\paragraph{Correctness.} The translation is compositional, and the exporter now keeps implicit
arguments and coercions, so that its declarations are closed and its applications match them.
But it leaves out universes, instances and computation rules, so the Dedukti code is not
type-checked (Section~\ref{sec:lean2dk}); a reading is as correct as the table entries it uses,
and the generated entries are checked only by the grammar. A check of the exported Dedukti
against its own signatures would catch errors like the shifted arguments of
\ttt{Equivalence.mk} automatically, and lean2dk's check of the same declarations would
guarantee what the readings say; a back-translation from the readings to Dedukti would close the
loop, as Informath supports in the other direction.

\section{Conclusion}

Symbolic informalization of Lean is feasible at the scale of Mathlib if the lexicon can be
inferred: Mathlib's naming conventions and docstrings, with the grammar's own lexicon, give wordings to 69\% of its
definitions, and a grammar can check them. With them, all of Mathlib's definitions have been
read in English, as formulas and in French, almost a third of them with no symbolic residue in
English, in an hour on a laptop; and 9,126 statements of its undergraduate core, with 7,658
proofs, in English and French (and Czech in an earlier build), 72\% of them fully worded. A translation for reading is
not a translation for checking: lean2dk keeps everything a checker needs and is unreadable,
ours keeps what a reader needs, and the comparison showed where we had kept too little, the
coercions and implicit arguments that a reader does not see but a statement may depend on.
A translation for reading is not one for writing either: Verbose Lean's controlled language is
written by students and checked by Lean, ours is generated from what was checked, and the two
meet where our proof texts are written in Verbose's language and checked again. Mathlib's own structure helps too: its docstrings name the
parent a textbook would, its \ttt{to\_additive} table gives the additive structures, and its
delaborator the notation, so that its classes read as definitions by extension. The readings are faithful by construction, and they
come in five languages at once. What the method does not give is judgement: which wording is
right, which word a language uses, when a reading is false although it parses. That remained
the human's part, in a collaboration where the AI agent did most of the construction and the
measuring.
